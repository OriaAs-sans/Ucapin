# UX Best Practices, Speaking Pedagogy and Gamification for a Speaking-First English Learning Website

Note on evidence quality: most findings below come from search-result abstracts and snippets. The only full page fetched was Duolingo's streak article. Effect sizes should be checked against the full texts before anyone quotes them as final.

## 1. What does SLA research say about effective speaking practice (shadowing, output, corrective feedback, spaced repetition of phrases, CEFR descriptors)?

### Takeaway
The strongest evidence supports three things. Explicit corrective feedback beats implicit recasts, especially "prompts" that push the learner to correct themselves. ASR-based pronunciation practice with explicit feedback has a medium-to-large effect. Repeating tasks and practicing formulaic sequences explicitly improves fluency. Shadowing looks promising but has no meta-analysis yet. CEFR's 2020 Companion Volume scores pronunciation by intelligibility, not by accent, which should shape how the app scores it.

### Cited Findings
**Corrective feedback (CF)**
- Lyster & Saito (2010), a meta-analysis in *SSLA* of 15 classroom studies (N = 827): prompts (elicitation, clarification requests and similar moves that push the learner to self-correct) had larger effects than recasts. The gap was biggest on free, constructed-response measures. Younger learners benefited more from CF. — [Cambridge Core / SSLA](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/oral-feedback-in-classroom-sla/4999EE1C8379B2BF026B148EAF373CA1)
- Ammar & Spada (2006): prompts were more effective overall. High-proficiency learners gained equally from recasts and prompts, but low-proficiency learners gained significantly more from prompts. — [Ammar & Spada PDF](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Ahlem-Ammar-2/publication/232021519_One_size_fits_all_Recasts_prompts_and_L2_learning/links/590885a0458515ebb49088ac/One-size-fits-all-Recasts-prompts-and-L2-learning.pdf)
- Brown (2016), a meta-analysis of what teachers actually do: recasts make up 57% of classroom CF and prompts 30%. Grammar errors get the most CF (43%). Teachers therefore default to the less effective type. — [Brown 2016 meta-analysis record](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/100066197262_0x000001)
- Lyster, Saito & Sato (2013) group oral CF into reformulations (recasts and explicit correction) and prompts. — [Lyster, Saito & Sato 2013](https://www.scribd.com/document/632285530/LysterSaitoSato2013)
- Caveat: CF has been the subject of about 18 separate meta-analyses, and their results depend on how each one defines its domain. — [search summary, McGill eScholarship](https://escholarship.mcgill.ca/downloads/6w924g64b)

**ASR and pronunciation feedback**
- Ngo, Chen & Lai (2024), a meta-analysis in *ReCALL* (15 studies, 38 effect sizes, 2008–2021): ASR had an overall medium effect on ESL/EFL pronunciation (g = 0.69). ASR with explicit CF was "largely effective"; indirect feedback such as ASR dictation was moderately effective. Effects were large for segmentals (individual sounds) and small for suprasegmentals (stress, rhythm, intonation). Medium-to-long treatments worked better than short ones. Practicing with peers worked better than practicing alone. Effects were strongest for adults and intermediate learners. — [ReCALL 2024, Ngo et al.](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/recall/article/effectiveness-of-automatic-speech-recognition-in-eslefl-pronunciation-a-metaanalysis/A915444CF252B61D14961D2FE733822D)
- A 2023 PRISMA review in *Languages* found that few tools use ASR for pronunciation assessment, and most focus on segmentals while neglecting intonation. — [ASR in L2 Learning PRISMA review](https://www.aminer.cn/pub/6585b29a939a5f40828b0e1b/automatic-speech-recognition-in-l-learning-a-review-based-on-prisma-methodology)

**Shadowing**
- An Oxford systematic review/dissertation: shadowing can improve comprehensibility, intelligibility and accentedness, plus suprasegmental control such as fluency and prosody. However, most studies used controlled tasks and few linked acoustic measures to real listener judgments. — [ORA Oxford](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:3104cae1-3a6b-400a-b173-de44384238d2)
- Small Indonesian quasi-experimental and pre/post studies report fluency gains from shadowing over standard teaching. — [ELTIN journal](https://e-journal.stkipsiliwangi.ac.id/index.php/eltin/article/view/7350); [UM Palembang](https://jurnal.um-palembang.ac.id/englishcommunity/article/download/9808/4487)
- A practitioner blog cites a 2025 *Language Teaching Research* systematic review on shadowing. I could not verify that review. — [Migaku blog](https://migaku.com/blog/language-fun/shadowing-a-practitioners-guide-to-the-technique-in-2026)

**Task repetition and formulaic sequences (spaced practice of phrases)**
- A 2025 *Language Teaching* study (52 Polish EFL learners): exact task repetition increased lexical diversity and marginally improved fluency. The benefit held regardless of how much learners enjoyed the task. — [Cambridge, Language Teaching 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/interaction-between-task-repetition-and-oral-task-enjoyment-effects-on-speech-complexity-accuracy-and-fluency/D8C1FD395F5275FF3F3AC2356FB15A45)
- A 2025 meta-analysis of task repetition in L2 writing (not speaking; 17 studies, N = 442): overall g = 0.25. A mirror copy reports fluency g = 0.51, but that figure is unverified. — [LTR TR meta-analysis PDF](https://todoele.net/sites/default/files/2025-12/LTR-effect-of-task-repetition-meta-analysis.pdf)
- A task-planning meta-analysis (oral): task repetition had a medium positive effect on syntactic complexity. — [Bohrium summary](https://www.bohrium.com/en/paper-details/task-planning-and-oral-l2-production-a-research-synthesis-and-meta-analysis/865005257623601348-9980)
- Baleghizadeh & Shafeie (2019; 48 intermediate Iranian EFL learners): spaced productive retrieval of formulaic sequences, after explicit teaching, produced a longer mean length of run (a fluency measure) than non-spaced retrieval or the control group. — [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/0a68cdfb2e0a4ee69f508431a74b8596)
- Nergis (2021, *Lingua*): advanced EAP learners who practiced 80 formulaic sequences over five weeks beat a single-word vocabulary group on pruned speech rate. The effect held at the delayed posttest. — [x-mol / Lingua](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1369426355859017728)

**CEFR speaking descriptors**
- The CEFR Companion Volume (2020) splits Phonological Control into three subscales: overall phonological control, sound articulation and prosodic features. It foregrounds intelligibility, not accentedness. — [CoE CEFR CV PDF](https://rm.coe.int/16809ea0d4); [Piccardo, new phonology scale](https://rm.coe.int/the-new-scale-for-phonological-control-piccardo-/1680788b29)
- The revision responded to criticism that the 2001 scale treated accent as a sign of poor control. — [DiSLaw journal](https://dislaw-journal.uibk.ac.at/ds/article/download/63/59/105)
- The CV also includes a separate "Spoken fluency" scale (p. 146 in one edition's table of contents). I did not retrieve its wording. — [CEFR schools Cyprus TOC](https://cefr.schools.ac.cy/downloads/CONTENTS%20p06-09.pdf); [CoE phonological competence page](https://coe.int/web/common-european-framework-reference-languages/phonological-competence)

### Inferences
- Default AI feedback should be a prompt first ("Try that again — listen to the stress on *-tion*"). Show an explicit correction or model answer only after a retry. A recast alone suits only higher-level learners. This maps onto a retry loop: attempt → targeted prompt → retry → model plus explanation.
- Score pronunciation on intelligibility and comprehensibility, in line with CEFR CV 2020, not on "native-likeness." Avoid labels like "accent score."
- ASR is reliable for segmentals but weak on prosody, so prosody feedback should lean on shadowing and listen-and-compare (the learner's waveform or pitch next to a model) more than on a numeric score.
- Peer practice outperformed solo ASR practice. That supports an optional peer or community layer, such as shared recordings with peer reactions, alongside private AI practice.
- Build a "phrase deck" of formulaic sequences (chunks) with spaced productive retrieval: learners say the phrase aloud in context rather than recognizing it. Pair it with task repetition (do the same speaking task 2–3 times, then show the fluency gain between attempts).
- Background theory that I did not source in this session: Swain's Output Hypothesis (output forces noticing gaps) and Long's Interaction Hypothesis. A writer should cite primary sources if these are used.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis of shadowing was found. Evidence comes from small studies plus one unverified 2025 review.
- No meta-analysis of oral task repetition specifically was found.
- I did not retrieve the exact CEFR CV spoken fluency descriptor wording. Use the CoE PDF directly.
- I did not find primary 2020–2026 sources on the Output Hypothesis.

## 2. How to reduce foreign language speaking anxiety (FLSA) in an app; evidence on AI chatbot speaking practice

### Takeaway
Systematic reviews (2024–2026) mostly report that AI chatbot practice increases speaking opportunities and lowers FLSA, partly because the bot is a "less critical audience." Evidence quality is mixed: one study found anxiety went up, and transfer to talking with real humans is unproven.

### Cited Findings
- A 2026 systematic review of 24 studies: chatbot practice improved vocabulary, grammar, fluency and pronunciation and reduced FLSA. Bots were described as a less critical audience. The abstract also calls itself a "meta-analysis," so its methods are unclear. — [Systematic review, AI chatbots & EFL speaking](https://diajeng.lldikti6.id/articles/18784)
- A PRISMA review of 13 articles: AI-supported speaking gives repeated practice opportunities and "may" reduce anxiety for some learners. — [UM conference paper](https://conference.um.ac.id/index.php/isolec/article/download/10667/4409)
- A 2024 study in *System* comparing conversational generative-AI chatbots: one experimental group showed significant gains in willingness to communicate (WTC) and self-perceived communicative competence (SPCC), plus lower FLSA, versus the control group. — [XJTLU record](https://scholar.xjtlu.edu.cn/en/publications/the-impact-of-different-conversational-generative-ai-chatbots-on-/)
- A six-week Mondly study: significant speaking gains and lower FLSA versus the control group. — [search summary record](https://harvester.ues.edu.sv/vufind/ProQuestFSGRecord/3235540124)
- Counter-evidence: in a Replika study, L2 speaking anxiety was higher after the intervention. — [search summary; see Polizzi review](https://cris.unibo.it/retrieve/handle/11585/1067810/629e689d-8f23-480a-a199-169f3fafb5e6/02%2BPolizzi%2B13-54.pdf)
- Polizzi et al.'s critical review argues the chatbot evidence needs re-evaluation and that the disadvantages are under-studied. Reviewers flag that transfer of gains to human interlocutors is an open question. — [Polizzi, Univ. Bologna](https://cris.unibo.it/retrieve/handle/11585/1067810/629e689d-8f23-480a-a199-169f3fafb5e6/02%2BPolizzi%2B13-54.pdf)
- Related: leaderboard and public-competition pressure correlated with performance anxiety and lower intrinsic motivation in an EFL writing study. — [search summary, Juperan journal](https://ojs.smkmerahputih.com/index.php/juperan/article/download/2650/2066/6155)

### Inferences
- Anxiety-reduction design levers:
  - Keep recordings private by default; sharing is explicit opt-in.
  - Present the AI partner as a non-judging practice buddy.
  - Offer a warm-up such as shadowing or reading a phrase aloud before free speech.
  - Let learners prepare: show the task, offer 15–30 s of planning time, and give a hint or sentence-starter button.
  - Allow unlimited retries and never show a public failure state.
  - Choose the feedback timing: delay corrections until after a conversation turn, or show them all in an end-of-session summary, so fluency isn't interrupted.
  - Keep leaderboards away from speaking quality.
- Use a graded exposure path: shadowing (lowest anxiety) → read-aloud → guided Q&A with the AI → open roleplay with the AI → optional peer or tutor sessions. This also targets the open transfer-to-humans question.
- Avoid "companion" or relationship-style bots. The Replika anxiety increase suggests a task-focused partner is safer, though the evidence is a single study.
- Measure WTC and self-reported anxiety in-app, for example a 1–5 confidence check after a session, to track whether the design actually lowers anxiety.

### Gaps
- I found no true quantitative meta-analysis (pooled effect size) on chatbots and FLSA.
- There is little longitudinal evidence (beyond 6–8 weeks) on AI speaking practice, and none on real-world transfer.
- I found no direct experimental evidence comparing "private vs. public" recording on anxiety in apps.

## 3. UX patterns for mic interaction on the web (push-to-talk vs. VAD, permission onboarding, recording states, retry, feedback display, errors)

### Takeaway
Ask for mic permission in context, after a clear explanation and a user action. A denial is hard to recover from. Push-to-talk (or tap-to-toggle) gives predictable turn boundaries and copes better with noise and pauses, which matters for learners who hesitate. VAD is hands-free but can clip speech or end a turn during a pause. Never use color as the only signal in pronunciation feedback.

### Cited Findings
**Permissions**
- Ask for mic access only when it is first needed. `getUserMedia()` triggers the browser prompt immediately if access hasn't been granted. — [web.dev, Recording audio](https://web.dev/articles/media-recording-audio)
- Once the user denies access, the site cannot ask again. Use the Permissions API to check status first; `getUserMedia` itself gives no status. — [web.dev, Recording audio](https://web.dev/media-recording-audio)
- The best time to ask is right after an explicit user action, such as clicking a button that needs the permission. — [web.dev, Permissions best practices](https://web.dev/articles/permissions-best-practices)
- Google Meet case study: an earlier pre-prompt with only a "Dismiss" button confused users, who then didn't grant camera or mic access. The redesign explains why access is needed. — [web.dev, Google Meet permissions case study](https://web.dev/case-studies/google-meet-permissions-best-practices)
- Safari's support for the Permissions API is reportedly incomplete, so a fallback is needed. This comes from a secondary source. — [Chrome tips article](https://chrome-tips.pages.dev/articles/chrome-permissions-api-camera-microphone)
- A practitioner case study on mic handling in a web voice app. — [Doist, "Building Ramble #1: Taming the Microphone"](https://doist-dev.pages.dev/building-ramble-1-taming-the-microphone)

**Push-to-talk (PTT) vs. voice activity detection (VAD)**
- PTT gives deterministic control over speech boundaries; the system isn't guessing when the user has finished. It maps to press-and-hold, toggle or keyboard shortcuts. With PTT, users can pause mid-utterance without triggering a response, which VAD can misread as the end of a turn. — [LiveAvatar docs, push-to-talk](https://docs.liveavatar.com/docs/full-mode/push-to-talk)
- VAD suits hands-free or ambient use; PTT suits controlled input and shared-mic settings. — [Convai docs](https://docs.convai.com/api-docs/plugins-and-integrations/convai-unreal-engine-plugin/getting-started/configure-conversation-input); [TeamSpeak support](https://support.teamspeak.com/hc/en-us/articles/360002745898-What-is-the-difference-between-Push-To-Talk-and-Voice-Activity-Detection)
- VAD's detection threshold can clip phrase onsets. PTT is cleaner in noisy environments. — [Voxbooster blog (anecdotal)](https://voxbooster.com/blog/discord-push-to-talk-vs-voice-activity-2026/)
- Patents describe hybrid modes: a short tap toggles recording, a long press works as hold-to-talk. — [USPTO patent 10999420](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10999420)

**Accessible feedback display**
- WCAG 1.4.1 (Level A): color must not be the only visual means of conveying information or distinguishing elements. Color coding is allowed if another cue backs it up, such as shape, icon or text. — [W3C Understanding SC 1.4.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/use-of-color)
- W3C failure F81: marking errors by color difference alone is a failure. WCAG 3.3.1 requires errors to be identified and described in text. — [W3C F81](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Techniques/failures/F81.html)

### Inferences
**Recommended mic interaction model**
- Use tap-to-start / tap-to-stop as the default for exercises, with hold-to-talk as an option and Space bar as the desktop shortcut.
- In AI conversation mode, offer VAD auto-end as an opt-in with a generous silence timeout (about 1.5–2 s or more for learners; my estimate, not sourced). Show a visible "still listening…" indicator and a manual "I'm done" button.
- Default to PTT or toggle on mobile and in noisy settings.

**Permission onboarding flow**
1. A "Mic check" step on first speaking use: explain why the mic is needed and that recordings stay private, with a single "Turn on microphone" button.
2. Call `getUserMedia` only on that click.
3. Run a quick level test ("Say 'Hello, my name is…'") with a live meter, and confirm that the mic is picking up the learner's voice.
4. If access is denied, show browser-specific steps for re-enabling it (lock icon → Site settings), plus a text-only fallback so the learner isn't blocked.

**Recording states to design**
- idle → requesting permission → ready → recording (live waveform or level meter, timer, pulsing red dot plus "Recording" text) → processing (spinner, "Analyzing…") → result → retry.
- Also design a max-length auto-stop and a "too short / no speech detected" state.

**Error states**
- No mic found, permission denied, mic in use by another app, too quiet, too noisy (signal-to-noise check, with a prompt to "move somewhere quieter or use headphones"), network or ASR failure (keep the recording locally and offer a retry).
- Every error state needs copy that tells the learner what to do next.

**Pronunciation feedback display**
- Show a word-level transcript with three bands: good, needs work and missed, using color plus icon or underline style plus a text label.
- Tapping a word plays the model audio next to the learner's own audio, with a tip on mouth position or stress.
- Give a summary score on the intelligibility scale.
- Limit corrections to 1–3 priority items per attempt to avoid overload. This follows from the prompt/CF findings; the limit itself is a design inference.

**Retry flow**
- "Try again" is always one tap away. Keep the best attempt, show the change between attempts (e.g., +12% clarity), and never shame repeated attempts.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed or NN/g usability study comparing PTT and VAD for language learners. Evidence is vendor docs and anecdote.
- I didn't fetch Apple HIG or Material guidance on audio recording. Their specifics are absent here.
- I found no sourced benchmark for learner-appropriate VAD silence thresholds.

## 4. Gamification: what works, what backfires; speaking-specific progress metrics

### Takeaway
Gamification reliably raises engagement and motivation, but its link to learning outcomes is weaker. Streaks work through habit and, later, loss aversion; flexibility (freezes) helps, and broken streaks demotivate. Leaderboards and badges can undermine intrinsic motivation and raise anxiety. For speaking, research supports speech rate, mean length of run and pause frequency as the fluency metrics that best match listeners' perceptions.

### Cited Findings
**Duolingo data (company-reported)**
- Allowing learners to equip two Streak Freezes raised daily active learners by +0.38% and did not increase days off. Post-lesson and milestone animations raised 7-day retention of brand-new learners by +1.7%. — [Duolingo blog, How the streak builds habit](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit)
- Learners who reach a 7-day streak are 3.6× more likely to complete their course. This is correlational. — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit)
- Duolingo's design lessons:
  - Early streak days feel proportionally bigger (2→3 is +50%), so celebrate early.
  - Loss aversion sustains long streaks.
  - Flexibility motivates more than rigid rules.
  - Breaking a streak can be demotivating, and fear of losing one may stop people from starting. — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit)
- Separating the daily goal from the streak: +3.3% Day-14 retention and +10.5% more daily learners with a streak of over 20 days. — [Duolingo blog, Improving the streak](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak)
- Streak Wager: learners offered it came back more often and completed more lessons. — [Duolingo Engineering](https://making.duolingo.com/how-streaks-keep-duolingo-learners-committed-to-their-language-goals). A secondary source reports the largest lift at Day 7 (+14%). — [Yu-kai Chou blog](https://yukaichou.com/gamification-study/master-the-art-of-streak-design-for-short-term-engagement-and-long-term-success/)
- Next-day retention went from 13% (2012) to 55% (2019), per a Bloomberg interview with Luis von Ahn, cited secondhand. — [Yu-kai Chou blog](https://yukaichou.com/gamification-study/master-the-art-of-streak-design-for-short-term-engagement-and-long-term-success/)
- Duolingo reportedly optimizes for CURR (current-user retention rate) rather than raw DAU. This comes from a secondary source. — [The Audiencers](https://theaudiencers.com/subscription-models-live-off-habits-lessons-from-duolingos-retention-success/); see also [Lenny's Newsletter, Duolingo streaks](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/behind-the-product-duolingo-streaks)

**Academic evidence**
- Hanus & Fox (2015, *Computers & Education*; N = 71, 16 weeks): a gamified course with leaderboards, badges and coins lowered motivation, satisfaction and empowerment over time. Lower intrinsic motivation in turn lowered exam scores. The authors attribute this to rewards for already-interesting tasks undermining intrinsic motivation. — [UCLA SMHP PDF](https://www.smhp.psych.ucla.edu/pdfdocs/gamil.pdf); [Hanus profile](https://osu.academia.edu/MichaelHanus)
- Mekler et al. (2017): points, levels and leaderboards raised the quantity of performance but had no effect on competence, autonomy or intrinsic motivation. They worked as extrinsic incentives. — [Mekler et al. 2017 PDF](https://glassmanlab.seas.harvard.edu/annotated_works/mekler17.pdf)
- A systematic review of 40 EFL/ESL studies (Zhang & Hashim 2023): frequent gains in motivation and engagement, but uneven effects on outcomes. — [search summary, Frontiers in Education](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2024.1371955/epub)
- A systematic review of 36 studies on gamified EFL motivation among undergraduates: it flags missing long-term evidence. — [IJERE](https://ijere.iaescore.com/index.php/IJERE/article/view/34101)
- A review of gamification for oral English skills (50 studies, 2019–2023): most report motivation gains that facilitate oral practice. — [Ciencia Latina](https://ciencialatina.org/index.php/cienciala/article/download/15332/21874?inline=1)
- A EUROCALL-era review (2011–2019): gamification was mostly positive for motivation and engagement, but few studies confirmed links to learning outcomes. — [CRIRES literature review](https://crires.ulaval.ca/publication/64b0809c0e710119ac43eb6a)
- A gamification meta-analysis on English proficiency in South Korea, with moderators such as gaming elements. I didn't retrieve its effect sizes. — [MDPI Sustainability 2023](https://www2.mdpi.com/2071-1050/15/14/11325)
- In an EFL writing study, leaderboard-driven peer pressure correlated negatively with intrinsic motivation and positively with performance anxiety. — [Juperan journal](https://ojs.smkmerahputih.com/index.php/juperan/article/download/2650/2066/6155)
- Practitioner commentary: streaks shift from pride to anxiety and obligation as they lengthen, and abandonment risk rises with perceived investment. — [Yu-kai Chou, streak design and burnout](https://yukaichou.com/gamification-analysis/streak-design-gamification-motivation-burnout/); [Screenwise parent guide](https://screenwiseapp.com/guides/managing-duolingo-streaks-when-gamification-becomes-stressful)

**Speaking metrics: what predicts perceived fluency**
- A meta-analysis (Cambridge) found speech rate (r = .76) and mean length of run (r = .72) are the strongest correlates of listener-perceived fluency, followed by articulation rate (r = .62). Pause frequency (r = −.59) beats pause duration (r = −.46). Repair measures are weakest. Speech rate explains about 58% of the variance. — [Cambridge Core meta-analysis](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/FAB18835B2117A299C33F8F502248C81)
- Temporal measures, except speech rate, depend on the pause threshold chosen. Non-native listeners are more lenient and rely less on speech rate. — [Suzuki PhD, Lancaster](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/154312/1/2021suzukiphd.pdf); [Interspeech 2025, Yazawa](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2025/yazawa25_interspeech.pdf)
- Mid-clause pause ratio separates mid from high fluency levels. — [JYX thesis](https://jyx.jyu.fi/bitstream/123456789/60156/5/Towards%20the%20phonetic%20basis%20of%20spoken%20second%20language%20assessment.pdf)

### Inferences
**What to use**
- Daily goal in speaking minutes, with small presets (e.g., 3 / 5 / 10 / 15 min).
- A streak with built-in flexibility: freezes, a weekend-off option or a "repair" path, and a weekly-streak alternative to reduce anxiety.
- Celebrate early streak milestones.
- XP tied to effortful speaking: XP for attempts and retries, not just correct answers, which rewards output.
- Mastery-style badges, e.g., "Mastered 50 phrases" or "First 5-minute conversation," rather than pure volume badges.

**Use with caution**
- Public leagues or leaderboards. If used at all, make them opt-in, small and similar-level, and based on effort (minutes spoken), never on pronunciation score.
- Loss-framed notifications ("your streak will die!").
- Anything that rewards quantity over quality, such as rapid-fire skipping to farm XP.

**Speaking progress metrics for the dashboard**
- Minutes spoken (daily, weekly, total)
- Words spoken
- Speech rate in words per minute, labelled for learners as "speaking speed"
- Mean length of run ("words between pauses")
- Pause frequency
- Pronunciation and intelligibility score trend
- Phrases mastered (from the spaced-retrieval deck)
- Conversations completed and CEFR-aligned can-do statements achieved
- Self-rated confidence trend

**Metric framing**
- Show personal trends ("vs. last week"), not comparisons with others.
- Use a fixed pause threshold consistently, e.g., 250 ms (a common research convention, not sourced here), so trends stay comparable.
- Don't present WPM as "higher = always better" beyond a natural range.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed study specifically on streak anxiety in language apps.
- I found no public Duolingo data on leagues' effect on learning outcomes, or on its speaking features (e.g., Video Call).
- I didn't retrieve native-speaker WPM norms for conversational English from a citable source.
- The Journal of Consumer Research (2023) study on streaks is mentioned in a blog, but I couldn't identify it.

## 5. Suggested information architecture and dashboard elements for the learner home page

### Takeaway
No direct empirical study of language-app home pages was found. The IA below is inferred from the evidence above: a habit loop (daily goal plus flexible streak), a single clear "speak now" action, personal speaking-progress trends, a review queue of spaced phrases, and private-by-default framing.

### Cited Findings
- Small, visible daily habits and a tangible streak counter encourage daily practice even in short sessions. — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit)
- Separating the daily goal from the streak improved retention. — [Duolingo blog, Improving the streak](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak)
- Longer treatments and peer practice improve ASR pronunciation outcomes. — [ReCALL 2024](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/recall/article/effectiveness-of-automatic-speech-recognition-in-eslefl-pronunciation-a-metaanalysis/A915444CF252B61D14961D2FE733822D)
- Spaced productive retrieval of formulaic sequences improves fluency. — [DOAJ, Baleghizadeh & Shafeie](https://doaj.org/article/0a68cdfb2e0a4ee69f508431a74b8596)
- Ask for the mic only when it's needed, in context. — [web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/permissions-best-practices)

### Inferences
**Proposed home dashboard, desktop layout (top to bottom; one column on mobile)**
1. **Hero card, "Today's speaking"**: a primary CTA ("Start 5-min practice") that picks the next best activity, a ring showing goal progress in minutes, and a streak flame with freeze count.
2. **Continue / path**: the current unit or scenario (CEFR level badge, e.g., "B1 · Ordering at a restaurant") with progress bar, and the next lesson.
3. **Phrase review due**: "8 phrases to review" (spaced repetition of chunks, said aloud).
4. **AI conversation partner**: quick-start roleplay topics, with a low-stakes label ("Private practice · no one else hears this").
5. **Progress panel**: weekly minutes spoken (bar chart); trend lines for pronunciation/intelligibility and speaking speed (WPM) or words between pauses; a count of phrases mastered.
6. **Feedback highlights**: the top 2–3 recurring issues ("/θ/ in *think*", "word stress in *-tion* words"), each with a one-tap drill.
7. **CEFR can-do tracker**: checklists such as "I can describe my weekend (A2)" ticked off by completed tasks, showing movement toward the next level.
8. **Achievements and optional community**: mastery badges, plus opt-in peer sharing or a small, effort-based league.

**Top-level navigation**
- Home · Practice (Shadowing, Pronunciation drills, Phrase review) · Conversations (AI roleplay, optionally peers or tutors) · Progress · Profile/Settings (mic test, feedback strictness, goal, notifications, privacy).

**Onboarding**
- Choose a goal (travel, work, exam) → self-assessed CEFR level plus a short speaking placement → a mic check with privacy explanation → a first win: a 30-second shadowing task with positive, specific feedback.

**Settings that support anxiety reduction and learner autonomy**
- Feedback timing (instant vs. end of session), correction intensity (light / standard / detailed), whether a score is shown, and the input mode (tap-to-talk, hold-to-talk or VAD).
